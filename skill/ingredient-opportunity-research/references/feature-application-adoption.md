# Feature-to-Application-to-Adoption Protocol

Use this protocol as the mandatory decision chain for market-feasibility and customer-discovery work.

## 1. Establish Ingredient Properties

Investigate properties that could create downstream value or block use:

- physicochemical: solubility, hygroscopicity, crystallization, molecular size, viscosity, water activity, phase behavior;
- processing: heat, pH, shear, fermentation, freeze-thaw, drying, storage, and packaging stability;
- sensory: sweetness, bitterness, odor, color, mouthfeel, and aftertaste;
- biological or functional: mechanism and measured efficacy relevant to the proposed use;
- compatibility: interactions with proteins, starches, lipids, hydrocolloids, surfactants, minerals, and other actives;
- safety and analytical: impurities, contaminants, assay method, identity, and dose constraints.

Use this evidence table:

`property | claimed value or risk | ingredient identity/grade | test matrix | dose | comparator | method | measured result | exact inline source and locator | source type | reading depth | transferability | limitation | evidence level`

The property table is for measured ingredient properties, not finished-product replacement performance. Do not put replacement percentage, incumbent comparison, finished-product sensory acceptance, commercial adoption, or formulation strategy in this table. Place those observations in the application-case, alternatives, effects, or replacement/co-formulation sections. A property may motivate an application, but the application result is not itself an ingredient property.

Prefer original studies and standards. Reviews identify research directions; retrieve original studies for decision-critical claims. Treat patents and supplier studies as useful but interested evidence. Record adverse properties and null results, not only benefits.

Require an auditable source in every row: author/body, year, short title, journal/standard, DOI or stable URL, and page/table/figure/section when available. Do not use a bibliography at the end of the report as a substitute for row-level attribution. When only an abstract, review citation, patent example, or supplier claim was read, say so in the row.

For each property, test both directions: where it solves the product problem and where the same property conflicts with another product requirement. An application map that records only advantages is incomplete.

## 2. Map Properties to Product Needs

Build the chain:

`supported property -> observable product/user problem -> required product function -> candidate application`

Do not treat semantic similarity as demand. “Moisturizing” can suggest a skincare application, but advancement still requires evidence that the ingredient works in a relevant formulation, at an achievable dose, under the target use conditions.

Use this gate table for food applications:

`supported property and source | product problem | required function | candidate application | need evidence | China regulatory gate and source | United States regulatory gate and source | European Union regulatory gate and source | technical gate | use amount and basis | use cost and basis | identifiable buyers | overall result | decisive evidence/gap`

Use a single regulatory column only for non-food applications or when the user explicitly limits the geography. Within each jurisdiction cell, write `pass`, `conditional`, `fail`, or `unresolved`, followed by the decisive category, condition, and source. A global `allowed` statement is not acceptable when one jurisdiction remains unresolved or excludes the population or product class.

Allowed results:

- `advance` — no decision-critical gate currently fails;
- `conditional—experiment required` — plausible, but a defined technical test is required;
- `regulatory unresolved` — official status or use boundary remains unclear;
- `technical evidence insufficient` — no adequate direct or adjacent evidence;
- `do not advance` — a gate fails under the stated scope.

Regulatory conclusions must specify jurisdiction, product class, route, target population, ingredient identity/grade, dose, production source or strain when relevant, salt/form, specification, and any authorization exclusivity or data-protection condition. Verify that the supplier's exact product can rely on the authorization; a same-named molecule or another producer's approval is not sufficient. “Allowed in food” does not mean allowed in infant formula, supplements, cosmetics, or every country.

For food ingredients, use the joint China–United States–European Union table in [regulatory-audit.md](regulatory-audit.md) and cite the applicable jurisdiction-and-category row in each application gate. Check China's current GB 2760 and other applicable approval routes, United States CFR/GRAS or other routes, and European Union additives/Novel Food or other routes. Report the regulator's original use unit and basis.

## 3. Verify Company and SKU Adoption

Before evaluating company adoption, compare the target ingredient with incumbent and credible alternative ingredient systems in each shortlisted application.

### Same-Application Alternative Comparison

Define the product matrix, processing conditions, target function, required outcome, and incumbent benchmark before selecting alternatives. Include alternatives that are currently used, technically capable of the same job, visible in relevant product labels, or commonly considered by formulators. Do not build a generic ingredient comparison detached from the application.

Compare on a functionally equivalent basis appropriate to the decision. Equal weight is not automatically comparable: normalize by the performance target or substitution basis relevant to the application, and disclose when the source used a different basis.

Evaluate the dimensions that can change the buyer's decision, including:

- use amount and achieved functional performance;
- range and tunability of product behavior;
- process, matrix, storage, packaging, and use-condition compatibility;
- sensory or user-facing consequences;
- interactions with other formulation components;
- regulatory, claim, specification, supply, and label implications;
- target and alternative ingredient-system use cost, switching friction, and—when a supportable finished-product formulation cost basis is available—the change in estimated total ingredient cost.

Use original comparative studies, standards, patents with worked examples, and method-disclosed application studies for technical comparisons. Supplier material can support a formulation hypothesis but remains interested evidence. Trade or media sources can support market adoption, buyer language, or launch context; they do not prove comparative technical superiority without a disclosed method.

Use this table:

`application/matrix | required function and benchmark | target ingredient/grade and comparison basis | incumbent or alternative | alternative-selection evidence | target-ingredient advantage and source | alternative advantage and source | trade-off/failure condition | use-amount basis | incumbent ingredient-system cost | target ingredient-system cost | ingredient-system cost change | estimated total ingredient cost before/after | total ingredient-cost change | technical transferability | label/regulatory implication | conclusion and validation gap`

Calculate cost on a functionally equivalent formulation basis. Show each retained, removed, reduced, or added ingredient, its use amount, price basis, yield/loss, and formula. If the full recipe and comparable prices are available, estimate total ingredient cost before and after and report both the absolute and percentage change. Call it `estimated total ingredient cost`, not total production cost; exclude labor, energy, depreciation, packaging, logistics, testing, compliance, and conversion unless separately evidenced. If the full recipe is unavailable, report only the ingredient-system cost change and name the missing denominator instead of inventing a percentage.

For every claimed target-ingredient advantage, check whether an alternative matches or exceeds it under another relevant condition. Report advantages of the alternatives and disadvantages of the target ingredient with the same evidence discipline. If sources are not like-for-like, state that the comparison is directional and design a controlled benchmark test rather than declaring a winner.

### Select Replacement or Co-Formulation Strategy

Do not assume the sales proposition is full replacement. Decompose the incumbent system into the product jobs it performs, then map which jobs the target ingredient can perform alone, which remain uncovered, and which may benefit from another ingredient or process change.

Consider the strategy supported by evidence:

- **full replacement:** the target ingredient can meet the decision-critical functions and constraints on its own;
- **partial replacement:** the target ingredient replaces part of the incumbent while the remaining incumbent retains necessary functions;
- **co-formulation:** ingredients perform complementary roles or the combination has directly measured interaction benefits;
- **process-enabled change:** the opportunity depends on changing order of addition, hydration, activation, thermal/shear treatment, or another process variable;
- **retain incumbent/no switch:** replacement or co-formulation has no supported value or introduces an unacceptable constraint;
- **unresolved—comparative experiment required:** the evidence cannot yet distinguish the strategies.

Search original mixture, substitution, formulation, rheology, sensory, stability, process, or application studies in the target or closest available matrix. Capture total functional-ingredient amount, component amounts and ratio, grade, co-ingredients, order of addition, hydration/activation conditions, pH/ionic environment or other mechanism-relevant variables, processing, storage, controls, endpoints, and measured results.

Use this table:

`application/matrix | incumbent product jobs | target-ingredient jobs | uncovered jobs | strategy | partner/incumbent retained | total amount basis | target/partner amounts and ratio | process conditions | comparator design | measured outcome | interaction/synergy evidence status | exact source/locator | transferability | regulatory/label/cost implication | risk | validation action`

Do not call a combination synergistic merely because it outperforms one component. Use `synergy` only when the study uses an appropriate additive or mixture benchmark, comparable total amount, and an analysis capable of demonstrating interaction beyond expected additivity. Otherwise report `combination performance observed; synergy unverified`.

Present literature formulations as evidence-backed options for user evaluation, not production recipes. Keep source formulation, proposed screening formulation, and validated commercial formulation separate. Check regulation, specifications, label effects, cost-in-use, supply, process complexity, sensory consequences, and IP constraints for every component and combination.

Identify leading companies using an explicit category basis such as sales, distribution, product leadership, innovation activity, or recognized market position. State the basis and data limitation.

Record the company-universe definition and SKU verification coverage: number of companies screened, number of relevant SKUs checked, channels and dates covered, and whether ingredient labels were accessible. Do not describe a list as “top companies” when selection mixes size, fame, innovation, and convenience without saying so.

### Adoption Evidence Ladder

| Class | Evidence | Permitted conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Current on-pack ingredient list or legible packaging image tied to an exact SKU/market; current regulatory/product filing; authorized structured label record | `verified current use` for that SKU, market, formulation version, and observation date |
| A2 | Current official brand product page with complete ingredient list, corroborated by a current authorized retailer or marketplace record | `verified current use`, with page-change and regional-formula limitations |
| A3 | Method-disclosed product database record with ingredient deck and launch/update date, such as Mintel GNPD or Innova, preferably corroborated by a live SKU | `verified at recorded launch/update`; confirm current sale before calling it current use |
| C1 | Current company filing, audited procurement disclosure, contract, import/manufacturing record, or authoritative statement naming the ingredient and period but not the SKU | `verified company-level use/procurement`; exact product adoption remains unresolved |
| D1 | Company or supplier launch announcement, worked formulation case study, patent example, R&D collaboration, regulatory submission, recruitment, or trial | `development evidence`; not commercial adoption unless a current SKU is identified |
| L1 | Distributor listing, marketplace title, seller copy, review mention, social post, search snippet, or generic company-category fit | `possible use` or lead only |

Recommended public search order:

1. official brand site and current package/ingredient image;
2. official or authorized flagship stores and major retailers with legible labels;
3. product or label registries relevant to the category and jurisdiction, including Chinese special-food/product filings when applicable, USDA FoodData Central Branded Foods for US packaged foods, and NIH Dietary Supplement Label Database for US supplements;
4. company filings, recalls, specifications, press releases, technical case studies, and supplier/customer announcements;
5. patents, recruitment, conference material, distributor pages, marketplace sellers, social posts, and search snippets as development or discovery signals.

Recommended paid sources are Mintel GNPD and Innova for ingredient-deck and launch screening. Use NielsenIQ, Circana, Euromonitor, platform analytics, or retailer panels for commercial continuation or sales signals, not as proof of the ingredient unless their record includes the SKU ingredient list.

Search exact ingredient names, synonyms, branded ingredient names, E/INS numbers, spelling variants, local-language names, and common OCR errors. Exclude easily confused substances. Save the exact label wording, URL, screenshot or record identifier when authorized, market, pack size, observation/update date, and whether the product is currently purchasable.

When retail or marketplace evidence is available, follow [ecommerce-label-research.md](ecommerce-label-research.md) for platform order, SKU inspection, capture fields, corroboration, and coverage reporting.

Classify adoption:

- `verified current use` — current label, ingredient list, official formulation, regulatory filing, or equivalent product-level evidence;
- `development evidence` — relevant patent, trial, R&D disclosure, collaboration, or pipeline evidence without verified commercial use;
- `possible use` — indirect evidence only;
- `no public evidence found` — absence of evidence, not proof of non-use;
- `confirmed absent/discontinued` — current authoritative evidence supports absence or discontinuation.

`No public evidence found` requires a coverage statement, not a bare conclusion. `Confirmed absent` applies only to the checked SKU/formulation when a current complete label omits the ingredient, or to discontinuation when the brand or another authoritative source states it. It does not prove that an entire company has never used the ingredient.

Use this adoption table:

`application | company | brand/SKU/market | adoption status | ingredient/alternative and exact label wording | evidence class | primary SKU-level evidence and date | corroboration | current-sale status | verification coverage/gap`

### Effect and Claim Evidence Boundaries

Evaluate five channels separately. Never merge them into one statement that the ingredient “works.”

1. **Technical effect:** finished-product stability, formulation, processing, sensory, or performance evidence. Prefer controlled tests in the exact matrix and process; record dose, comparator, metric, time point, result, and transferability.
2. **Human/use effect and adverse effects:** clinical, instrumental, controlled consumer-use, or field evidence appropriate to the category. Record the exact ingredient/product, population, amount and duration, comparator, endpoints, adverse events, withdrawals, and null results. Proactively search tolerability and foreseeable harms using risk terms derived from the ingredient's mechanism, exposure route, intended use, target population, regulatory assessments, known class effects, and emerging evidence.
3. **Regulatory claim eligibility:** determine whether the finished product—not the ingredient in isolation—can legally use the proposed wording in each target jurisdiction. Record the exact claim, product-category conditions, compositional threshold, required analytical evidence, mandatory qualifiers/warnings, and source version. Eligibility is permission to make a claim; it is not proof of technical, human, consumer, or commercial effect.
4. **Consumer evidence:** reviews, complaints, preference tests, repeat-purchase signals, and expressed experience. Record the exact SKU/platform/market, sample and denominator, date window, selection and coding method, observed positive and negative themes, and comparator if any.
5. **Commercial evidence:** sales or GMV trend, distribution, listing duration, SKU expansion, repeat orders, and continued brand investment. Record the metric definition, geography/channel, data source or estimation method, baseline/comparator, period, and promotion or seasonality limitations.

For every consumer or commercial conclusion, apply this **SMART audit**. This is an evidence-completeness check, not a score:

- **S — Specific:** exact ingredient, SKU/product, market/channel, population or customer, and outcome;
- **M — Measurable:** metric, unit, sample/denominator, and baseline or comparator;
- **A — Auditable and attributable:** reproducible source/method and explicit attribution boundary; observational association must not be written as ingredient causality;
- **R — Relevant:** evidence matches the target matrix, population, buying decision, or channel;
- **T — Time-bound:** study/observation period and source access date are stated.

Use `pass` or name the exact gap for each SMART element. Do not publish bare statements such as “consumers like it,” “good repeat purchase,” or “commercially successful.”

Use a separate claim table:

`jurisdiction | proposed claim | exact finished-product threshold/conditions | final-product evidence available | permitted wording/status | mandatory qualifier/warning | official source/version/date | remaining gap`

Use a separate effects table:

`effect channel | exact claim/outcome | object/SKU/market | metric/method | sample/denominator | comparator/baseline | time window | exact source/date | SMART check | observed result | attribution allowed | limitation`

Sales, reviews, claims, patents, and continued listing cannot isolate the ingredient's causal contribution. Eligibility to make a regulatory or marketing claim describes a compliant finished product under stated conditions; it does not establish technical, human, consumer, or commercial effect. Say what is observed, what may be associated, and what remains inference.

## 4. Convert Evidence to Customer Discovery

After applications pass the gates, identify:

- verified users with second-source, supply-risk, cost, performance, or molecule-expansion potential;
- users of substitutes with a supported switching proposition;
- non-users whose specific products have a documented need matching the ingredient property.

Every account must trace to a relevant product or active development signal. Company size or broad category membership alone is insufficient.
