# Food Terminology and Language Protocol

Use this protocol for Chinese or bilingual food-ingredient deliverables. The objective is technically natural target-language writing, not literal sentence-by-sentence translation.

## Establish Identity and Working Terminology

Before drafting, identify the researched ingredient unambiguously: preferred local name, English name, abbreviation when relevant, exact identity/grade/source, and the easily confused substance that must be excluded. Maintain a working terminology table internally only when needed:

`concept/entity | preferred Chinese term | English term | abbreviation | exact identity or scope | authoritative usage source | accepted synonym | misleading or forbidden rendering | report usage note`

Prioritize terminology found in current Chinese regulations and standards, regulator publications, Chinese Pharmacopoeia or relevant industry standards, authoritative Chinese textbooks or dictionaries, and peer-reviewed Chinese literature. Preserve the original legal term when discussing a foreign authorization and explain its Chinese meaning instead of silently replacing it with the nearest Chinese regulatory concept.

Distinguish molecular family, individual molecule, salt or form, production organism or source, commercial grade, process, mechanism, technical function, physiological outcome, product claim, and marketing expression. Similar-looking terms are not interchangeable.

## Writing Rules

- On first use, write the preferred Chinese term followed by the English term and abbreviation when this improves auditability. Thereafter use one stable term.
- Preserve source wording for legal categories, official claims, analytical endpoints, units, study scales and named technical methods. Translate the explanation, not the underlying category or measurement.
- Do not translate an English modifier into a stronger Chinese conclusion. Keep distinctions such as association versus causation, tolerance versus safety, intended use versus authorized use, and mechanism versus demonstrated outcome.
- When no authoritative Chinese equivalent is found, retain the original term, provide a short operational definition, and label the translation provisional.
- Keep article and regulation titles in their original language when needed for retrieval; a Chinese explanatory title may follow.
- Do not use English sentence order, stacked noun phrases, unexplained abbreviations, or abstract verbs that make Chinese read like machine translation. Rewrite the paragraph around the decision question in natural professional Chinese.

## Final Language Audit

Run a separate target-language editing pass after the evidence and conclusions are fixed:

1. identity consistency: one entity is not given multiple unexplained names;
2. regulatory consistency: official categories, claims and legal effects are not colloquially paraphrased;
3. technical consistency: matrix, process, use amount, endpoint and effect terms retain their scientific meaning;
4. claim-strength consistency: the Chinese wording is no stronger than the source evidence;
5. readability: each sentence has a clear subject and decision purpose, and literal English syntax is removed;
6. retrieval: decision-critical translated terms remain traceable to the original source term.

The default report should contain one concise ingredient-identity statement, not a standalone terminology section. Show a terminology table only when multiple names, translations, molecular family members, grades, sources, legal categories, or claims could materially change identity, regulatory status, evidence transfer, or the decision. If terminology remains disputed or market-specific, show the alternatives and state which one the report adopts. Do not hide uncertainty behind fluent wording.
